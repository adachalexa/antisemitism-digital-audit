Introduction & Background

Since October 7, 2023, U.S. campuses have seen a sharp increase in both reported antisemitic incidents and student activism around Israeli-Palestinian policy. Campus leadership has largely relied on faculty reports, incident logs, and legacy media — while students experience the issue through search algorithms, social feeds, and algorithmic amplification of outside political voices. This audit bridges that gap with three primary data streams analyzed across a structured AI stress test protocol.

The Problem from the Provost's Perspective

The institution faces a "vocabulary gap" and "information gap" between how students and faculty/administrators understand key terms: antisemitism, anti-Zionism, decolonization, apartheid, and "Free Palestine." Policies written in one vocabulary are being applied to a campus community operating in another — and the emotional narrative shaping student understanding is being delivered by CNN, PragerU, and TikTok influencers, not campus communications.

Data Sources & Scope

Three primary datasets: Google Trends (YouTube, Web, and News search, 2021–2026), Brand24 social listening (Instagram, TikTok, YouTube — North America, March 15–April 13, 2026, 562 mentions, 6.6M reach), and Comscore audience data (February–April 2026). All three datasets were analyzed through a structured AI Stress Test Logger applied to Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini.

How a Word Spreads — The Three-Stage Journey

Not every term makes it all the way through

For a political term to shape public understanding, it has to pass through three stages. First students discover it — usually through a video or creator. Then they look it up to understand what it means. Then journalists start reporting on it and it becomes part of the official conversation. Most terms in this dataset never make it to stage three — and one never even made it to stage one.

Stage 1
Discovery
Students first encounter the word — usually through YouTube, music, or a creator they follow
Stage 2
Understanding
Students Google it — searching "what does this mean?" or "is X the same as Y?"
Stage 3
Official Discourse
Journalism and institutions adopt the term — it appears in news, policy, and campus communications
Antisemitism
Complete journey
Reached students

Kanye West and Kyrie Irving made antisemitic statements in 2022 — millions searched the term that same week. Students learned about it through celebrity controversy, not news coverage.

Reached students

After hearing the word, students searched "what is antisemitism" and "antisemitic meaning" — query growth of +2,750%. They were trying to understand something that went viral before they had context.

Reached institutions

Journalism, universities, and Congress all adopted this term. It now appears in Title VI guidance, ADL reports, campus conduct codes, and news coverage — the only term in this dataset to complete all three stages.

Anti-Zionism
Stalled at stage 2
Reached students

A YouTube debate between two commentators (the Munk Debate, 2023) introduced this term to a large audience. Students encountered it through a video, not a news article. Before Oct 2023 it had essentially zero search history.

Reached students

After encountering the term, students searched "is anti-Zionism antisemitism?" and "what is anti-Zionism" — these became some of the fastest-growing search queries in the dataset (Breakout status = >5,000% growth).

Never reached journalism

Despite widespread student use, U.S. journalism almost never adopted this term as a primary frame. It appears in news search data in only 2 states across 5 years — meaning campus policies and news coverage rarely use the word students are actually searching.

Decolonization
Stalled at stage 2
Reached students

Students encounter "decolonization" in two places: their history classrooms (AP World History) and activist content on YouTube. Search spikes every April — the end of the spring semester — confirming this is a campus-driven term.

Reached students

Students Google it frequently — sometimes as a homework question, sometimes trying to understand its political meaning. "Decolonize Palestine" searches grew +700%. The same word is being searched for three completely different reasons.

Never reached journalism

Despite being widely used in student and activist spaces, this term almost never appears in news searches. Journalism did not adopt it as a frame for covering the conflict — so when students use it in protests or complaints, administrators may not recognize its meaning.

Apartheid
Never left stage 2
Never reached students

Zero YouTube search signal in 48 of 50 states across the full 5-year window. Despite being used by Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, and debated in international courts, students never searched for this term on YouTube — because it never traveled through video or creator content.

Reached a small group

A small audience — primarily people already following NGO reports and human rights organizations — did search this term on the web. But it never broke through to a general student audience.

Never reached journalism

Absolute zero in news search data for 60 consecutive months. This is the most important finding in the dataset: the term that institutions adopted most loudly is the one the public ignored most completely — because it traveled through written reports, not through video.

Free Palestine
Almost complete
Reached students

Spread nationally through music and social media creators — IShowSpeed, Macklemore, Rachel Zegler. Present in 49 of 50 states. Students heard this phrase through artists they follow, not through political news.

Reached students

After hearing it in songs and videos, students searched "why is everyone saying free Palestine?" and "what does free Palestine mean?" — showing it became culturally ubiquitous before most people understood what it referred to politically.

Partial — and declining

Journalism covered it primarily in the context of protests — but that coverage has declined by 70%. The cultural saturation never fully translated into an established journalistic frame, so campus policies treat it inconsistently.

AI Stress Test Finding — Google Trends (All 3 AIs Consistent, No Material Conflicts)

All three systems agreed: a pop culture controversy (Kanye/Kyrie, Oct 2022) drove more antisemitism searches than the October 7 attack (index 100 vs. 35). Claude uniquely identified this as the most analytically significant finding — not raised by ChatGPT or Gemini. The definitional Breakout queries ("what is anti-Zionism," "anti-Zionism vs antisemitism") show students are in a genuine learning phase, not uniformly committed to either view. The Google Trends findings are corroborated by the Brand24 data: the +2,483% anti-Zionism mention surge confirms the vocabulary shift from near-zero is real, sustained, and accelerating.

Leadership Interpretation

Gen Z College Students are only 2.9% of the measurable U.S. adult internet audience (7.07M of 244.2M). The properties that over-index for this group — NYT (30%), BBC (14.1%), Inside Higher Ed (12.5%) — are not where anti-Zionism content generates engagement. Anti-Zionist content on Instagram generates 60× the social actions of antisemitism-awareness reporting posts. Antisemitism conversation is declining (–31%) while anti-Zionism is surging (+2,483%). These two conversations are happening on entirely different platforms, in entirely different emotional registers, with almost no overlap — and campus leadership's communications infrastructure is built for the declining one.

Recommendations for Campus Leadership

Three specific, realistic actions grounded in the data above. Each addresses a documented gap between the information ecosystem students inhabit and the institutional infrastructure campus leadership has built.

Communication
01

Start Communicating Where Students Actually Are

Right now, the conversation about antisemitism and anti-Zionism on campus is happening almost entirely on TikTok and YouTube — not in email inboxes, not on news websites, and not through official university channels. Nearly three quarters of all tracked posts about these topics appeared on TikTok alone.

The voices shaping how students feel about this issue are outside commentators and media personalities — CNN, PragerU, political YouTubers — not university administrators. Each of these accounts posted just once and still reached hundreds of thousands of students through their follower base. Campus leadership needs to create short, clear video content that speaks directly to students in the spaces they actually use, and partner with student organizations that already have an established audience online.

Why the data supports this: posts about anti-Zionism grew by over 2,000% in a single month while posts about antisemitism declined by 31%. The conversation is moving fast, in a new direction, on platforms that university communications are not currently present on.
One thing to consider: engaging with politically charged language online carries reputational risk. The safest approach is to work with students across different viewpoints when developing content, and to make clear that the goal is understanding — not taking sides.
Policy & Training
02

Update Campus Policies to Reflect How Students Actually Talk About This

Students are not using the same words that campus policies were written around. Terms like "decolonization," "apartheid," and "anti-Zionism" are widely used in student conversations — but are largely absent from university conduct codes, incident-response procedures, and staff training materials. This gap means that when incidents occur, the people responding may not fully understand the language involved or the distinction students are drawing.

Our data shows that students are actively searching for the difference between antisemitism and anti-Zionism — meaning many are genuinely trying to understand where the line is, not deliberately trying to cross it. Policies and training programs that acknowledge this vocabulary, and that give staff clear guidance on how to distinguish protected political speech from targeted harassment, will be far more effective than those that ignore it.

Why the data supports this: anti-Zionism content carries significantly more negative emotional tone than antisemitism content and is growing rapidly — but it appears almost exclusively on social media, not in news coverage. Staff trained primarily on news-based definitions of these terms will be unprepared for what students are actually encountering and expressing online.
One thing to consider: some staff and legal advisors may be cautious about engaging with activist vocabulary. The key framing is that understanding a word is not the same as endorsing it — and understanding it is necessary for responding to it appropriately.
Infrastructure
03

Set Up a Simple System to Monitor What Students Are Seeing Online

Several of the most significant findings in this report — a 3,000% surge in campus Jewish student posts, a spike of 60 posts in a single day, anti-Zionism growing from near-zero to hundreds of posts in weeks — happened quickly and without warning. A basic ongoing monitoring system would have caught all of these early, giving leadership time to prepare a response rather than react to one.

This does not require surveillance of individual students. It means regularly tracking publicly available data on social media — what terms are trending, what tone those conversations are taking, and which outside voices are reaching the most students. This research project is itself a model for how that kind of monitoring can work. Additionally, because students increasingly use AI tools like ChatGPT and Gemini to understand contested topics, providing campus-vetted definitions of key terms gives students a reliable reference point rather than leaving them to navigate inconsistent AI-generated answers.

Why the data supports this: when we asked three different AI systems the same questions about this data, they gave meaningfully different answers — and two of them gave answers with factual errors. Students using these tools to research antisemitism or anti-Zionism may be receiving inaccurate information without knowing it.
One thing to consider: any monitoring program needs to be clearly focused on public platform signals, not individual students. Publishing the methodology openly helps address concerns about privacy and surveillance.

Conclusion

Here is the central finding of this entire report, stated plainly: students and campus leadership are not just disagreeing about events — they are operating with completely different information. They are encountering this topic through different platforms, in different language, and through different voices. And that gap is not a feeling or an impression. It is measurable.

Consider what the data showed about the word "apartheid." Every major international human rights organization used it to describe the conflict. It was debated in international courts. And yet it produced zero search activity on YouTube in 48 out of 50 states over five full years. The word that institutions were loudest about is the word students most completely ignored — because students don't encounter political vocabulary through reports and press releases. They encounter it through videos, creators, and algorithms.

At the same time, the word "anti-Zionism" went from almost no online presence to over 300 posts reaching 4.4 million people — a growth of more than 2,000% in a single month. That conversation is happening on TikTok, driven not by students talking to each other, but by outside media personalities and political commentators with large followings who each posted once. The tone of that conversation is increasingly hostile. And none of it is being shaped by campus communications.

Students are not uninformed about this topic. They are heavily informed — just by a completely different set of sources, using a completely different vocabulary, in a completely different emotional register than what most campus policies and communications assume. The three recommendations in this report — communicating on the platforms students actually use, updating policies to reflect the language students actually use, and setting up a basic system to monitor what students are actually seeing — are all designed to close that gap before the next incident makes it unavoidable.

The question is not whether campus leadership needs to engage with what students are seeing online. It is whether to do so now, with evidence in hand — or later, after another incident, without it.
