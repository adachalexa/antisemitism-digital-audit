The Generation Gap: How College Students Understand Antisemitism and Anti-Zionism

"Why do college students frame antisemitism and anti-Zionism differently than previous generations — and what does the digital evidence suggest about how that gap emerged?"

A generation is learning about one of the world's most contested conflicts almost entirely through YouTube and social media — and as a result, they're developing a completely different vocabulary than the one journalism uses to cover the same conflict. This audit maps that gap so campus leadership can respond with clarity, not reaction.

Section II

Introduction & Background

Since October 7, 2023, U.S. campuses have seen a sharp increase in both reported antisemitic incidents and student activism around Israeli-Palestinian policy. Campus leadership has largely relied on faculty reports, incident logs, and legacy media — while students experience the issue through search algorithms, social feeds, and peer influencers. This audit bridges that gap. The central finding is not that students are uninformed. It is that they are informed differently, by different institutions, in a different language — and the gap between those languages is now measurable.

The Problem from the Provost's Perspective

The institution faces a "vocabulary gap" and "information gap" between how students and faculty/administrators understand key terms: antisemitism, anti-Zionism, decolonization, apartheid, and "Free Palestine." Policies written in one vocabulary are being applied to a campus community operating in another — producing miscommunication, conflict escalation, and institutional credibility loss.

Data Sources & Scope

Evidence is drawn from three primary datasets: Google Trends (YouTube, Web, and News search filters, 2021–2026), Brand24 social listening data (75M total reach, March–April 2026), and Comscore audience and content engagement data (February–April 2026). AI stress testing was conducted across Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini using a structured question logger applied to all three datasets.

Team 1 · Google Trends · YouTube / Web / News Search · United States · March 2021–March 2026

The Signal: Three Platforms, Three Vocabularies

The same five terms were tracked across YouTube search, general web search, and Google News search over sixty months. The three platforms are not different windows onto the same behavior — they are structurally different modes of engagement with political vocabulary. AI stress testing across all three CSVs confirmed the Google Trends findings with consistent agreement: the terms are moving in fundamentally different directions, responding to different triggers, and being searched by different audiences.

AI Stress Test Finding — Google Trends (All 3 AIs Consistent)

All three AI systems agreed on the core finding: a pop culture controversy (Kanye/Kyrie, Oct 2022) drove more antisemitism searches than the October 7 mass-casualty attack (index 100 vs. 35). Claude added the most analytically significant detail: anti-Zionism had essentially zero search history before this period, meaning every Breakout query represents a structural vocabulary shift, not just a spike. The definitional search queries — "what is anti-Zionism," "anti-Zionism vs antisemitism" — confirm students are in a genuine learning phase about these distinctions, not uniformly committed to either view. Campus programming that provides factual definitional frameworks could address a measurable informational gap.

AI Stress Test Finding — Brand24 (All 3 AIs Consistent, Claude Most Granular)

All three AI systems agreed that TikTok dominates campus-relevant conversation and that anti-Zionism carries higher proportional negativity than antisemitism (38% vs 24%). Claude uniquely identified that U.S.-filtered campus conversation about Jewish student life is the only dataset with clearly positive majority sentiment — a finding that directly contradicts the assumption that campus conversations about these topics are uniformly hostile. Claude also identified the 24% stable negative rate in "direct" filtered content as a structural pattern, not a one-time spike. Campus leadership that assumes all online discourse about antisemitism is crisis-level has not filtered for campus-specific context.

Leadership Interpretation — Comscore + AI Stress Test

The audience actively following antisemitism coverage in traditional/legacy media is more likely aged 25–54 than 18–24. Gen Z College Students represent only 2.9% of the measurable U.S. adult internet audience (7.07M of 244.2M). The properties that over-index for this group — NYT (30%), BBC (14.1%), Inside Higher Ed (12.5%) — are not where antisemitism-related content generates its highest engagement. Anti-Zionist and pro-Palestinian content on Instagram generates 50–80× the social actions of antisemitism-awareness reporting posts. Campus-age students are algorithmically more likely to encounter pro-Palestinian framing than antisemitism-as-hate-crime framing — and when they turn to AI for definitional clarity, they receive inconsistent answers.

Section IV

Recommendations for Campus Leadership

Three specific, realistic actions grounded in the data above. Each addresses a documented gap between the information ecosystem students inhabit and the institutional infrastructure campus leadership has built. Each includes a foreseeable trade-off.

01

Recalibrate Institutional Communication Channels

The university must meet students where they are — TikTok and YouTube, not press releases and email. The Brand24 campus/youth data is unambiguous: zero Twitter/X, zero Facebook, zero news sites appear in the campus-filtered datasets. The conversation is entirely on video and visual platforms.

Develop short-form explainer content that directly addresses contested terms in clear, non-punitive language. Institutional silence on terms like decolonization, apartheid, and anti-Zionism does not signal neutrality — it signals irrelevance. U.S. campus content about Jewish student life is already 39% positive in the Brand24 data; build on that foundation.

Evidence tie-back: Anti-Zionism is 69% TikTok-concentrated with zero news coverage. Official campus statements on these terms are entering an information environment the institution does not currently monitor or participate in.
Trade-off: Engaging contested vocabulary carries reputational risk. Mitigation: co-develop content with student advisory groups across the political spectrum; frame all engagement as comprehension, not position-taking.
Communication
02

Update Dialogue & Incident-Response Frameworks

Any training, dialogue, or incident-response protocol that ignores the decolonization and anti-Zionism vocabulary will miss how students are actually thinking. Google Trends confirms this vocabulary has been building for five years through academic curricula and creator content — it predates any single campus incident and will not recede.

The Comscore data includes a Haaretz-cited UK campus survey (n=1,000, Jan–Feb 2026) showing nearly 1 in 4 British students witnessed behaviors targeting Jews on campus. Retrain Title VI officers with a contextual decision framework that accounts for both documented campus antisemitism and the distinct vocabulary communities that frame the conflict differently.

Evidence tie-back: TikTok sentiment on this topic scores 29% positive — the same topic runs 88% negative in news coverage. Students and administrators are experiencing the same conflict in fundamentally different emotional registers, not just different words.
Trade-off: Some faculty and legal counsel may resist engaging activist vocabulary. Mitigation: frame as comprehension, not endorsement — understanding a vocabulary is not the same as adopting it as institutional policy.
Policy & Training
03

Build an Ongoing Digital Intelligence Capability

Use this project's data workflow as a prototype for a standing early-warning tool. The Brand24 data shows micro-accounts with as few as 44 followers generating millions of views through algorithmic amplification — the velocity at which vocabulary spreads through the 18–24 information ecosystem means reactive institutional response is structurally too slow.

Establish light-touch governance around AI use on campus. The AI stress test documents that students receive structurally different answers to the same questions depending on which system they use. ChatGPT was non-citable on methodology questions; Gemini had partial inaccuracies. Providing institutionally vetted resources for contested terms is a low-cost, high-impact intervention.

Evidence tie-back: The U.S. campus Jewish Students dataset went from 2 mentions to 67 in one reporting period (+3,250%) — a sudden activation event that a monitoring capability would have detected early.
Trade-off: Ongoing monitoring raises surveillance concerns. Mitigation: monitor public signals only; publish methodology transparently; distinguish monitoring from tracking individuals.

Section V

Conclusion

Apartheid is the most revealing gap in this entire dataset. Every major human rights organization applied it to the conflict. International courts debated it. And it produced zero YouTube search signal across five years — in 48 of 50 states. The term that institutions most loudly adopted is the term the public most completely ignored — because it traveled through written reports, not through video. That inversion is not an accident. It is the clearest statement the data makes about how vocabulary actually moves in 2026.

Students and campus leadership are not just disagreeing about events. They are operating with different information diets, different vocabularies, and different emotional frames — all produced by digital ecosystems that institutional leadership does not currently monitor. Anti-Zionist content generates 60× more social engagement per post than antisemitism-awareness content on the same platforms. U.S. campus conversation about Jewish student life is 39% positive — a fact the aggregate data obscures entirely. Micro-accounts with 44 followers are reaching millions of students. And when those students turn to AI for definitional clarity, they receive structurally inconsistent answers.

The question for campus leadership is not whether to engage with this ecosystem. It is whether to engage intentionally, or by default — with evidence, or without it.

Not that young people are uninformed. That they're informed differently, by different institutions, in a different language — and the gap between those languages is now measurable. That's what this report documents. That's what leadership now has the evidence to act on.
