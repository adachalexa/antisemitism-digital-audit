The Generation Gap: How College Students Understand Antisemitism and Anti-Zionism

"Why do college students frame antisemitism and anti-Zionism differently than previous generations — and what does the digital evidence suggest about how that gap emerged?"

A generation is learning about one of the world's most contested conflicts almost entirely through YouTube and social media — developing a completely different vocabulary than the one journalism uses to cover the same conflict. The emotional narrative students encounter online is being shaped primarily by outside political and media voices delivered through algorithms, not peer-to-peer conversation.

Section II

Introduction & Background

Since October 7, 2023, U.S. campuses have seen a sharp increase in both reported antisemitic incidents and student activism around Israeli-Palestinian policy. Campus leadership has largely relied on faculty reports, incident logs, and legacy media — while students experience the issue through search algorithms, social feeds, and algorithmic amplification of outside political voices. This audit bridges that gap with three primary data streams analyzed across a structured AI stress test protocol.

The Problem from the Provost's Perspective

The institution faces a "vocabulary gap" and "information gap" between how students and faculty/administrators understand key terms: antisemitism, anti-Zionism, decolonization, apartheid, and "Free Palestine." Policies written in one vocabulary are being applied to a campus community operating in another — and the emotional narrative shaping student understanding is being delivered by CNN, PragerU, and TikTok influencers, not campus communications.

Data Sources & Scope

Three primary datasets: Google Trends (YouTube, Web, and News search, 2021–2026), Brand24 social listening (Instagram, TikTok, YouTube — North America, March 15–April 13, 2026, 562 mentions, 6.6M reach), and Comscore audience data (February–April 2026). All three datasets were analyzed through a structured AI Stress Test Logger applied to Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini.

AI Stress Test Finding — Google Trends (All 3 AIs Consistent, No Material Conflicts)

All three systems agreed: a pop culture controversy (Kanye/Kyrie, Oct 2022) drove more antisemitism searches than the October 7 attack (index 100 vs. 35). Leadership Interpretation

Gen Z College Students are only 2.9% of the measurable U.S. adult internet audience (7.07M of 244.2M). The properties that over-index for this group — NYT (30%), BBC (14.1%), Inside Higher Ed (12.5%) — are not where anti-Zionism content generates engagement. Anti-Zionist content on Instagram generates 60× the social actions of antisemitism-awareness reporting posts. Antisemitism conversation is declining (–31%) while anti-Zionism is surging (+2,483%). These two conversations are happening on entirely different platforms, in entirely different emotional registers, with almost no overlap — and campus leadership's communications infrastructure is built for the declining one.

Section IV

Recommendations for Campus Leadership

Three specific, realistic actions grounded in the data above. Each addresses a documented gap between the information ecosystem students inhabit and the institutional infrastructure campus leadership has built.

01

Recalibrate Institutional Communication Channels

The Brand24 data is unambiguous: the campus conversation about antisemitism and anti-Zionism is happening almost entirely on TikTok (73% of mentions) and YouTube (18%). Zero Twitter/X, zero news sites appear in campus-filtered datasets. The accounts driving the most reach — twodangerbrothers, Destiny, PragerU, CNN, Bill O'Reilly — posted only once each and reached hundreds of thousands through follower size alone.

This means the emotional narrative students receive is being set by outside voices, not campus communications. Develop short-form video content that directly addresses contested terms in clear, non-punitive language. Co-create with student organizations that already have established followings — including Hillel, which appears as a co-hashtag in the data.

Evidence tie-back: Anti-Zionism went from 12 mentions to 310 (+2,483%) in a single period. Antisemitism is simultaneously declining (–31%). Campus communications built around the antisemitism frame are chasing a conversation that is moving to a different platform in a different vocabulary.
Trade-off: Entering contested vocabulary terrain carries reputational risk. Mitigation: co-develop content with advisory groups across the political spectrum; frame all engagement as comprehension, not position-taking.
Communication
02

Update Dialogue & Incident-Response Frameworks

The AI stress test confirmed that students querying AI about "antisemitism vs. anti-Zionism" receive different answers depending on which system they use — including factually inaccurate ones. The Google Trends data shows the definitional search queries ("what is anti-Zionism," "anti-Zionism vs antisemitism") are Breakout terms, meaning students are actively seeking clarity on these distinctions.

Any incident-response protocol that treats antisemitism and anti-Zionism as equivalent — or ignores the decolonization/apartheid vocabulary — will misapply to the situations it encounters. The two datasets are moving in opposite directions on opposite platforms. Retrain Title VI officers using the specific vocabulary students are actually using, grounded in this dataset.

Evidence tie-back: Anti-Zionism carries 35% negative sentiment (vs. 24% for antisemitism), is 4× more engaging per mention, and is growing at +2,483% — while being entirely absent from news coverage. The institutional vocabulary gap is measurable and widening.
Trade-off: Some faculty and legal counsel may resist engaging activist vocabulary. Mitigation: frame as comprehension, not endorsement — understanding a vocabulary is not the same as adopting it as policy.
Policy & Training
03

Build an Ongoing Digital Intelligence Capability

The Brand24 data shows the U.S. campus Jewish Students dataset went from 2 mentions to 67 in one period (+3,250%). The anti-Zionism dataset surged from 12 to 310 (+2,483%). April 14 produced a single-day spike of ~60 mentions — 34× the daily average — suggesting a discrete triggering event. A standing monitoring capability would have detected all three signals early.

The AI stress test itself demonstrates the need for institutionally vetted resources. Students receive structurally different — and sometimes inaccurate — answers depending on which AI system they query. Providing campus-specific definitional guidance for contested terms is a low-cost, high-impact intervention that directly addresses a documented information gap.

Evidence tie-back: The stress test found ChatGPT non-citable on Comscore methodology, platform-ranking errors on Brand24, and both ChatGPT and Gemini overstating conflation conclusions the data does not support. Students using these tools for research are receiving inconsistent information.
Trade-off: Ongoing monitoring raises surveillance concerns. Mitigation: monitor public platform signals only; publish the methodology transparently; distinguish monitoring from tracking individuals.

Section V

Conclusion

Apartheid is the most revealing gap in this dataset. Every major human rights organization applied the term to the conflict. International courts debated it. And it produced zero YouTube search signal across five years — in 48 of 50 states. The term that institutions most loudly adopted is the term the public most completely ignored, because it traveled through written reports rather than through video. That inversion is not a data artifact. It is the clearest statement the evidence makes about how vocabulary actually moves in 2026.

Meanwhile, anti-Zionism mentions surged +2,483% in a single period — from 12 mentions to 310, generating 4.4 million in reach with 461,000 social interactions. The conversation is 73% TikTok, driven by outside voices with large followings posting once and reaching hundreds of thousands. Negative sentiment is growing as volume concentrates. And Talkwalker data on X shows 71.6% negative sentiment among 18–24 year old users. Students are not developing this vocabulary through peer conversation. They are receiving it algorithmically, from CNN, PragerU, and creators with 750,000 followers — and they are receiving it in an increasingly hostile register.

The question for campus leadership is not whether to engage with this ecosystem. It is whether to engage intentionally, with evidence, or by default — after the next incident, without it.

Not that young people are uninformed. That they're informed differently, by different institutions, in a different language — and the gap between those languages is now measurable. That's what this report documents. That's what leadership now has the evidence to act on.
